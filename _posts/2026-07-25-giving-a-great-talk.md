Robert Spekkens' PIRSA talk [Tips for giving a great talk](https://pirsa.org/22100109) is the kind of resource I like because it treats scientific speaking as a craft. A good talk is not simply a compressed paper, nor a display of how much the speaker knows. It is an act of communication shaped around an audience.

The central discipline, I think, is selection. A talk has to decide what it is really about: the one question, distinction, example, or result that should remain with the listener afterwards. Many talks fail not because the material is weak, but because the speaker tries to preserve too much of the paper's internal architecture.

This is especially important in foundational and philosophical work, where confusion can easily arise from terms that are familiar but used differently by different communities. A good talk should not merely state definitions; it should help the audience feel why the distinctions matter.

The best talks often leave me with a clearer question, not only with more information. That is a useful standard to aim for.

Source: Robert Spekkens, [Tips for giving a great talk](https://pirsa.org/22100109), Perimeter Institute, October 17, 2022. DOI: [10.48660/22100109](https://doi.org/10.48660/22100109).
