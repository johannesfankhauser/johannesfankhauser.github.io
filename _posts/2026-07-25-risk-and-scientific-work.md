George Musser's FQxI article [Risky Business: How Science Plays Things Too Safe](https://qspace.fqxi.org/articles/284/risky-business-how-science-plays-things-too-safe) describes a familiar tension in academic life. Science celebrates boldness in retrospect, but often rewards caution in advance. Funding, hiring, publishing, and peer evaluation all tend to favor projects that are legible, feasible, and likely to produce visible output.

The uncomfortable part is that this is not simply the fault of one villainous institution. Researchers, journals, funders, and departments all participate in the same incentive structure. Risk is admired when it succeeds, but failure remains difficult to narrate professionally.

This seems especially relevant for foundational work. Some questions are slow, conceptually awkward, and difficult to package as guaranteed deliverables. But if science only supports paths whose value is already obvious, it risks selecting against precisely the forms of inquiry that could change the background picture.

The difficult ideal is not risk for its own sake. It is risk with standards: speculative enough to matter, rigorous enough to deserve trust.

Source: George Musser, [Risky Business: How Science Plays Things Too Safe](https://qspace.fqxi.org/articles/284/risky-business-how-science-plays-things-too-safe).
